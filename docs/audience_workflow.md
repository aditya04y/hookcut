# Which promo should the editor test first?

Two openings can both read well on paper. One can hold a stranger for three seconds while another gives a returning viewer a reason to start the show. HookCut uses the screenplay to suggest cuts, then uses playback to update the posting order for a particular audience. One editor can run the whole workflow.

## Try the decision in two minutes

Start Streamlit, choose **Demo run (no API calls)**, and open **Audience studio**. Leave the goal on **Start the show**, then switch the audience from **new** to **returning**. The fixture is an original four-episode story. Its audience activity is simulated and the cohort preferences are deliberately built into the simulator.

| Opening | New viewers who held for 3s | New viewers who started the show | Returning viewers who held for 3s | Returning viewers who started the show |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Her ex bought the theater loan (C0001) | 38 / 47 | 16 / 47 | 26 / 49 | 17 / 49 |
| A key leads to a hidden recording (C0003) | 20 / 44 | 7 / 44 | 39 / 48 | 26 / 48 |

The denominator in every column is impressions. The reveal ranks first for new viewers and the mystery ranks first for returning viewers. Inspect either cut to see the underlying scene, which other cuts informed its forecast, and how many people actually reached the ending. C0006 has zero impressions. Its estimate comes from other cuts in the selected audience. The workbench also finds two different openings for the same ending in episode 3 that an editor could test against each other.

## How the pieces connect

1. **Activity matrix.** One settled impression records a pseudonymous viewer, audience cohort at the time, exact cut, seconds played from the start, and whether the viewer started the story from the end card. Skips contribute to the denominator. The viewer-by-cut matrix distinguishes an observed skip from a cut the viewer never saw. Each cut key binds the show, played script and ordered time ranges, so a change in candidate numbering preserves its history. Renaming the show creates a new namespace.
2. **Content genome.** Each cut links to its opening, ending, speakers, explicit relationships and story cues such as money at stake, an identity question or rising suspense. Each edge points back to timed script beats and identifies its source. Hook labels and unanswered questions come from the saved analysis. English wording rules and timestamps supply additional features. The feature table groups actual inputs by story element and cohort, so an editor can see where viewers dropped out. A single cut can contribute to several features.
3. **Recommendation model.** Choose three-second holds or story starts per impression. The direct count for a cut is smoothed toward a prior derived from its audience and up to five cuts with similar story features. Similarity uses weighted feature overlap, giving opening features more weight for holds and story stakes and endings more weight for starts. The prior contributes at most 20 equivalent impressions. A cut with no exposures can borrow evidence from related cuts. With no audience data, the existing editorial order is used. The model reorders the shortlist within each posting wave so late spoilers keep their scheduled place.
4. **Editor toolkit.** One screen loads the run, accepts playback, recomputes rankings, shows source beats and compares cuts. The model-routing view records which configured providers performed analysis, cold-viewer review and writing. Event aggregation, graph construction, forecasting and evaluation run locally. Editors can choose an opening, export a timeline, and bring back impressions from the test.

## Two activity grains and one interpretable model (`hookcut/engagement.py`)

The studio above works at promo grain. The engagement layer adds the episode grain ("tracking the hooks and the cliffs") and relates both to story features with a model an editor can read.

| Grain | One row is | Columns | Missing cell means |
|---|---|---|---|
| promo (viewer × cut) | a settled impression (`audience.Exposure`) | impression_id, viewer_id, cohort, clip_key, watch_seconds, continued, source. held_3s = watch_seconds ≥ 3; story_start = continued | the cut was never shown; a skip is an observed 0 |
| episode (viewer × episode) | an episode offered to a viewer (`engagement.Session`) | viewer_id, cohort, episode, started, watch_fraction, completed, continued_next, exposure, source | never offered; started=0 is an observed skip of the offered next episode |

`continued_next` means the viewer started episode n+1 within the attribution window. `exposure` is the impression_id whose story start opened the session, which links the two grains. Importers reject impossible rows (completion below 90 % watched, continuation off the last episode, mixed sources, conflicting repeats).

**Features.** Each episode gets a fixed core of features from the saved analysis: opening hook and ending cliffhanger scores, unresolved questions, promo-ready beats, tension, romance and thriller shares of the strong beats, late or missing pickup of the previous cliffhanger, largest spoiler, position in the season and an optional paywall flag. Each cut gets its hook and cut-point scores, length, hook family, context need, multi-moment structure, rising suspense, visible payoff and spoiler level from the candidate and `genome.build_genome`. When `story_graph.episode_features` is available, its numbers (open loops carried, new characters, tropes) are added as extra features.

**Model.** Four L2 logistic regressions in numpy: 3-second hold per impression, story start per impression, episode completed per started session, and next episode per completed session. Each outcome has a pooled fit with cohort intercepts and one fit per cohort (all cohort × feature interactions). Intervals are 90 % bootstrap intervals that resample whole cuts or episodes, because every session of an episode shares its features. Effects are reported as log-odds and as points of probability at the base rate, for a no→yes change or a two-standard-deviation swing. Extra features are tested one at a time on top of the core model. Fitting all ~50 story-graph numbers at once on 50 episodes over-fitted and lost to the base rate on held-out episodes.

**Decisions.** (1) Per-cohort promo ranking = (hits + m × prior) / (impressions + m), m ≤ 20. The prior is the cohort's model prediction from genome features, so a cut with no impressions still gets a score. The same estimator is available in the studio (`build_studio(prior="genome")`, or the "Story-feature model" option); the default stays on similar cuts. (2) Retention curves per cohort, endings that keep viewers (by cliffhanger score, open questions and question kind), the episodes the model expects to lose the most viewers with the two features driving each loss, and candidate paywall points in the 5–15 window, cross-checked with `report.season_summary`. (3) An A/B design with a sample-size calculation (described below).

**Simulator and its checks (SIMULATED).** One `PLANTED` dict defines four cohorts (new, returning, romance-first, thriller-first) with their own preferences, plus fatigue, an optional paywall, per-cut and per-episode noise and a seed. Viewers see six random cuts each. A story start opens the show, and returning viewers resume mid-season. Two checks follow. First, *planted recovery*: the estimated effects are compared with those the planted model implies on the same rows, which tests the pipeline and not any audience. Second, an *episode holdout*: five interleaved folds, where a cut is never trained on with any episode of its test fold.

On the real 50-episode run (`--simulate`, seed 7): 4,000 viewers × 133 cuts (24,000 impressions, 4.5 % dense, 13 cuts never shown) and 2,358 viewers × 50 episodes (16,468 cells, 2,208 of them observed skips). Planted signs were recovered for 95–100 % of effects, with a rank correlation of 0.72–0.99 over the planted effects. The planted value fell inside the 90 % interval for 76–98 % of them. 3–6 of 20–75 null features showed an interval excluding zero, about the rate chance predicts. One example is a "grief cue" on 3-second holds, a reminder that single-tag effects are hypotheses for a test. On held-out episodes the model beat the base rate on all four outcomes, but only slightly (continuation Brier 0.080 vs 0.083, AUC 0.67), because most of each outcome is coin-flip noise. A paywall placed on one episode cannot be separated from that episode's own noise, and its interval reaches zero. Learning the paywall effect needs a paywall-position test.

Run it: `.venv/bin/python -m hookcut.engagement --run outputs/run_full --simulate --out outputs/run_full` (add `--paywall 12` to plant one). For uploaded data, use `--events promo.csv --sessions episodes.csv`. The run writes `engagement.json`, plus 1,500-row samples of the simulated rows when the activity is simulated. Reported rows never leave viewer-level samples behind.

## What the evidence establishes

In the synthetic demo there are 160 viewers and 640 impressions. The episode holdout trains each forecast using only cuts whose episodes are disjoint from the target. A cross-episode edit therefore cannot leak a shared episode into its own training set. The comparison uses Brier score, the mean squared error of a predicted probability against the observed binary outcome. Lower is better. For story starts across both demo cohorts, the genome model scores **0.214** and the audience-average baseline scores **0.213**. The demo shows that the decision can respond to behavior. Nothing here, simulated or demo, is a measured lift on Kuku viewers.

An observed association between a family reveal and story starts can reflect who saw it, where it ran, or which other story elements appeared alongside it. To test the opening, assign eligible viewers randomly to two openings with the same ending, within one audience and placement. Keep assignment stable per viewer. Compare three-second holds and starts per impression with a common outcome window. To study an ending, keep the opening fixed, vary the ending, and examine starts per impression alongside starts among those who finished. The second metric diagnoses the ending and is sensitive to who reached it.

The two-openings test is sized in `engagement.json`. Detecting +3 points on a 54 % three-second hold needs about 4,300 viewers per arm (two-sided α 0.05, power 0.8). Detecting +2 points on an 11.5 % story start needs about 4,300 as well. The windows are: hold within the impression; story start within 24 h; episode 2 and episode 5 within 7 days, joined through the session `exposure` id. The first step toward explaining success across titles would be to repeat those tests on multiple shows, link starts to episode-two retention, and hold out entire titles. The current evidence covers cuts from one loaded show at a time.
